# 5. Spouse Claims, Patiently

## Sit down, we'll take this slowly

Here is the gentle premise: Social Security benefits can grow out of
**someone's work record** — and a spouse (or a divorced spouse, or a
young child) may be able to receive benefits **based on that record**
even without their own. If this chapter is the first time you've heard
that sentence, breathe. Thousands of households meet it at the same
kitchen table you're sitting at, usually while a spouse's retirement
letter makes everyone curious at once. Nothing about it requires
deciding anything today; this chapter only explains the shape.

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
benefits advice.*

## Riding on a record: the plain-English shape

- When a worker claims retirement or disability benefits, "other family
  members may also be eligible for benefits" on the same record — that
  sentence is SSA's, from *Understanding the Benefits*.
- For a spouse, the classic shape is **age 62 or older**.
- Separately: a spouse may qualify **at any age** while caring for a
  child on the record who is younger than 16 or has a disability.
- Family amounts are described by SSA as "up to half" of the worker's
  benefit amount for each qualifying family member — and SSA enforces a
  total **family maximum** that "varies but is generally about 150% to
  180%" of the worker's benefit. Your own family's exact amount is one
  of the things a real claim (not this guide) will compute.
- A spouse whose own work history earned a benefit usually receives
  **their own benefit plus any difference** that would make the
  combined amount larger — SSA checks that math during the claim.

## The divorced-spouse rules, plainly

From the same official publication — *Understanding the Benefits* — an
ex-spouse may qualify for benefits on the worker's record if:

- the marriage lasted **at least 10 years**,
- the divorce was **at least 2 years** ago when the worker has not yet
  filed for benefits themselves,
- the ex-spouse is **at least 62**,
- is **unmarried**, and
- is not entitled to a benefit on their own work record equal to or
  higher than the amount on the worker's record.

It can feel strange to have a benefit that depends on a past marriage.
It also usually feels better than expected: SSA's rules handle the
details so the two households don't have to negotiate anything with
each other, and the worker's own benefit does not shrink when an
ex-spouse draws on the record.

## The timing words, without the panic

- A spouse benefit can be **reduced** if the spouse claims **before
  full retirement age**; full retirement age is between 66 and 67
  depending on the birth year, and each person's own age is shown in
  their Social Security Statement.
- Spouse applications follow **Form SSA-2**, whose official rules let
  you apply **online if you are within 3 months of age 62 or older**,
  otherwise by phone or at a field office.
- There is genuinely **no penalty for asking.** One of SSA's own
  publications (aimed at women, useful for everyone) says it plainly:
  *"Ask a Social Security representative to explain the options so you
  can decide which would be best for you."* That's the whole decision
  protocol: ask, hear the options, decide slowly.

## The public-pension update worth knowing

For years, a pension from government work not covered by Social
Security could offset spouse's and survivor's benefits (the
"Windfall Elimination Provision" and "Government Pension Offset"). If
you've heard doom about those acronyms, update the folder: per SSA's
own publications, the **Social Security Fairness Act** ended WEP and
GPO — their last month was **December 2023**, and the rules no longer
apply to benefits payable for **January 2024 and later**.

## A worked pencil-sketch (no dollars, just structure)

Imagine Rosa, 64, whose spouse Amit claims his own retirement benefit.
Why Rosa's claim usually comes next:

- Amit records his claim; Rosa's eligibility grows from **the same
  record** (that sentence earns the marriage certificate its keep).
- Rosa reviews estimates with Amit's Statement and her own — both close
  at hand — and asks SSA to walk through what age-62 claiming would
  mean versus claiming at, say, her full retirement age.
- Rosa applies through the **same three doors** as any other claim
  ([chapter 4](04-phone-field-online.md)); online is available because
  she's within 3 months of 62.
- The claimant whose name is on the marriage certificate — that's
  information SSA will confirm — brings her birth certificate, the
  marriage certificate, and the rest of the SSA-2 documents list from
  [chapter 3](03-document-grab-list.md).

Rosa's numbers never appear in this guide; they live in her letters.
The pencil-sketch only shows that the sequence is: **claim first,
understand second, decide slowly third.**

## What this chapter is not

It is not advice about when *you* should have a spouse claim, when to
claim, whether to claim, or which combination is better. Those are
protected conversations between you, SSA (1-800-772-1213 or your field
office), and estimates from your own
[Statement](https://www.ssa.gov/myaccount/). This guide supplies
vocabulary, not verdicts — and proudly so.
