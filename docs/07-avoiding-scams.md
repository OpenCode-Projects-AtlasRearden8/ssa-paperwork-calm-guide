# 7. Avoiding Impersonation Scams

## Why Social Security has its own scam chapter

Impersonation of government agencies is a busy industry. The Federal
Trade Commission counted over **396,000 government-imposter complaints
in one recent year**, with reported losses above **$442 million** (SSA
cites the FTC's figures to raise awareness). The scariest thing to know
is also the most useful: **you already know how to spot these calls**,
once someone shows you the pattern. This chapter is that someone.

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
benefits advice.*

## The three golden rules

1. **Real problems arrive as letters.** SSA's own FAQ says that if
   there is a problem with your Social Security number or record, SSA
   "will typically mail you a letter." A stranger's phone call is not
   paperwork.
2. **Never read your Social Security number to someone who called
   you.** Treat it exactly like a bank-card number. If *you* placed
   the call to 1-800-772-1213, sharing what's needed to verify your
   identity with a real representative is ordinary. On an **incoming**
   call, it never is.
3. **The government does not accept gift cards.** If anyone anywhere
   mentions gift cards, wire transfers, prepaid debit cards, internet
   currency, or mailing cash — the conversation has already told you
   who they are not.

## SSA's official never-list

This is the precise set of promises SSA publishes about itself, in
several FAQs and blog posts:

SSA **will never**:

- threaten you with arrest or legal action;
- suspend your Social Security number;
- demand immediate payment from you;
- require payment by cash, gift card, pre-paid debit card, internet
  currency, or wire transfer;
- ask for gift card numbers over the phone, or that you wire or mail
  cash;
- ask for personal details or banking information "to give you a
  cost-of-living adjustment";
- promise a benefit increase or other assistance **in exchange for
  payment**;
- demand secrecy in handling a Social Security–related problem;
- send official letters or reports containing your personal
  information by email;
- text or email images of an employee's official government
  identification (impostors have fabricated badges — real employees
  do not phone in badge photos).

Two **actually yes** facts, also official: SSA *does* sometimes call,
but only when you recently applied, when you currently receive payments
and your record needs updating, or when you asked for the call; and
SSA sends text messages **only if you opted in** (plus account-security
codes for your my Social Security account).

And on money genuinely owed to SSA: real collections come by **letter**,
with payment options and appeal rights — and SSA accepts payments
electronically through **Pay.gov**, **Online Bill Pay**, or by check or
money order through the offices. Nothing legitimate arrives as a
rushed phone call about digital coins.

## The impersonator's toolkit, named

| Prop | How it sounds | The tell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The frozen number | "Your Social Security number has been suspended because of a crime" | SSA's never-list; numbers cannot be suspended |
| The urgent arrest | "Federal officers are coming unless you pay right now" | SSA never threatens arrest; urgency is the costume |
| The fake badge | A texted or emailed photo of an "official ID" | SSA never sends badge images |
| The spoofed caller ID | The phone says a plausible government name or a real SSA number | Caller ID can be faked ("spoofed"); hang up and dial 1-800-772-1213 yourself |
| The cold-collaborator | "I'm helping process your COLA; I just need your bank details" | SSA never asks for banking information to give you a COLA |
| The lookalike site | Web addresses that *rhyme* with the real one — think designs like "ssa-gov" slotted into fraudsters' favorite patterns | The real site is **www.ssa.gov** — type it yourself, don't click from a message |
| The let-me-pay-you | "Pay a fee and I'll release/increase/unfreeze your benefits" | Benefits are never released for a fee; applying is free |

## First aid if a call already happened

Please don't feel foolish if one of these got past you — these scripts
are built by professionals. The next steps go in order:

1. **Stop paying, keep every piece of paper or message in your
   [binder's](08-claim-binder.md) scam tab.**
2. **Report it to SSA's law-enforcement arm** — the Office of the
   Inspector General — at <https://oig.ssa.gov/>. It takes ordinary
   details, in ordinary language.
3. **Report other (non-SSA) government impersonations** to the FTC at
   <https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/>.
4. **Call the real number yourself**: dial 1-800-772-1213, tell the
   representative what happened, let them check your account for
   changes; write down the representative's name.
5. **Check the broader paperwork** — untouched accounts maybe; bank
   and card statements; — and if money or card details changed hands,
   call your bank. Banks have scripts too, kinder ones than you fear.

## Scam-proofing checklist

Copy this into your binder, or hand it to the kind relative who
answers scam calls on family group chats:

```
[ ] Incoming calls with "problems": I say "I'll call you back"
      and hang up. I dial 1-800-772-1213 myself.
[ ] I never read my Social Security number to somebody who called
      me first.
[ ] Gift cards, wire, crypto, prepaid cards, mailing cash: no - ever.
[ ] Nobody from any agency texts me badge photos, or emails me
      letters with personal details; if someone claims otherwise,
      that is the evidence, not the reason to comply.
[ ] I only reach ssa.gov by typing www.ssa.gov myself.
[ ] Payment I really owe SSA arrives by LETTER, and goes through
      Pay.gov, Online Bill Pay, or a mailed check/money order.
[ ] Every call gets one line in my call-log worksheet: date, time,
      name, subject, next step.
[ ] If I'm unsure, I take what the caller said and verify it,
      slowly, on my own terms.
```

## The mindset that beats the whole industry

Every legitimate Social Security fact survives a two-week delay. Every
fact that dies under a two-week delay was never a fact — it was a
pressure tactic. And a _kind_ answer exists to all of them: "I need to
think about that. I'll call the agency myself." Then hang up kindly —
the people you love will thank you for the sentence, and so will the
version of you who found the calm line in your binder when it mattered.
